For Candidates #

0.0 # Role Summary

  1. 0.0.1 # The Speaker of the Board is a Neutral Officer chiefly responsible for chairing meetings of the Board of Directors, but also for interpreting Bylaws and Rules of Order, overseeing and supporting other Neutral Officers, and other impartial duties.

0.1 # General Responsibilities

  1. 0.1.1 # The Speaker organizes and chairs all meetings of the Board of Directors, and chairs General Meetings, in accordance with their binding interpretation of the Bylaws, the Standing Rules, and Robert's Rules of Order.
  2. 0.1.2 # The Speaker oversees the Ombudsperson and Chief Returning Officer, and takes over their responsibilities in certain situations.
  3. 0.1.3 # The Speaker has other responsibilities listed in Chapter 2 of this Manual.

0.2 # Impact to Students

  1. 0.2.1 # The Speaker ensures that business before the Board of Directors proceeds in an orderly fashion and thereby enables EngSoc's benefits to reach students.
  2. 0.2.2 # The Speaker prevents the Engineering Society from violating its Bylaws or otherwise acting unfairly towards its Members (the students).
  3. 0.2.3 # The Speaker supports the Ombudsperson and Chief Returning Officer in bringing fair complaint resolution and elections, respectively, to the student body.

0.3 # Required Qualifications

  1. 0.3.1 # The Speaker must be impartial in all aspects of the role.
  2. 0.3.2 # The Speaker must be familiar with the Bylaws of the Society.

0.4 # Useful Qualifications

  1. 0.4.1 # Skills and/or experience in the following are beneficial for the Speaker to have:
    1. 0.4.1.a # Meetings with formal rules of order, e.g. Model United Nations
    2. 0.4.1.b # Attendance at meetings of the Board of Directors
    3. 0.4.1.c # Chairing meetings
    4. 0.4.1.d # Policy interpretation
    5. 0.4.1.e # All qualifications mentioned in the Ombuds Manual, section 0.4
    6. 0.4.1.f # All qualifications mentioned in the Returning Manual, section 0.4
  2. 0.4.2 # Knowledge of the workings of the Engineering Society is an asset.

0.5 # Role Takeaways

  1. 0.5.1 # A Speaker missing qualifications listed in section 0.4 may gain them by exercising the role.
  2. 0.5.2 # The Speaker role is useful experience for a prospective candidate for President to have.

2.0 # Ongoing Responsibilities

  1. 2.0.1 # The Speaker chairs each Board and General Meeting, including without limitation:
    1. 2.0.1.a # maintaining the orders of the day as prescribed in the Bylaws, the Standing Rules, and Robert's Rules of Order (the latter two summarized in Chapter 3)
    2. 2.0.1.b # barring Directors of the Board from voting on motions where the member has a conflict of interest
    3. 2.0.1.c # stepping down as chair for the duration of the motion at hand if, in their judgement or on a motion passed by the meeting, a substantive conflict of interest arises between their duties as Speaker and the motion at hand
  2. 2.0.2 # The Speaker has regular duties at each Board Meeting, as outlined in section 2.2
  3. 2.0.3 # The Speaker has regular duties at each General Meeting, as outlined in section 2.4
  4. 2.0.4 # The Speaker investigates all formal complaints against Directors of the Board (including Officers), and all complaints which the Ombudsperson is unable to investigate themselves, as described in the Policy on Complaints.

  8. 2.0.8 # The Speaker calls Special Meetings of the Board of Directors in accordance with Bylaw 1, section 4.8.

    1. 2.0.9.a # Orders the Webmaster or Sysadmin to provide access to the VP Communications Google account,
    2. 2.0.9.b # Calls a Special Meeting of the Board of Directors to appoint Acting Vice Presidents for all vacant VP positions, using the VP Communications account to send notice of the meeting,
    3. 2.0.9.c # Ensures that the Chief Returning Officer arranges a snap by-election (or adds the Officers to any shortly-upcoming election), and uses the VP Communications account to send out election notices as applicable,
    4. 2.0.9.d # Performs the minimum number of actions as Interim President necessary to maintain the Society's operations until a new Officer team is elected.

EngSoc's Rules of Order for New Chairs #

3.0 # Components of a Meeting

  1. 3.0.1 # The assembly, that is, the voting members of the meeting. Throughout this summary we will assume this is the Board, but this also applies to General Meetings, where the voting members are the EngSoc Members present at the meeting, and any references to "the Board" or "Board member" can be replaced by "the present Members" or "present Member" for a General Meeting.
  2. 3.0.2 # The chair or presiding officer, that is, the person running the meeting. In this context, this is always the Speaker of the Board (elected or interim).
  3. 3.0.3 # The minutes, that is, the official record of what occurred at the meeting. In this context, minutes are always taken by the VP Communications.

3.1 # Motions

  1. 3.1.1 # In general, a motion is any action which the Board wishes to take. If an EngSoc Member obviously wishes something to happen, but does not express it in the form of a motion, the Speaker should nevertheless interpret it as a motion and help the Member decide on the precise type and wording of the motion.
  2. 3.1.2 # Motions come in several main types:
    1. 3.1.2.a # Main motions, that is, the actual business before the Board. Things like "approve the Finance Committee recommendations" or "recall a Project Director". See section 3.4
    2. 3.1.2.b # Subsidiary and incidental motions, that is, actions taken on another motion or which arise from another motion. Things like "amend the motion", "suspend the rules and do something", or "vote now". See section 3.5
    3. 3.1.2.c # Privileged motions, that is, actions which impact the rights of Members. Things like "take a recess" or "speak louder". See section 3.6
  3. 3.1.3 # Most motions have:
    1. 3.1.3.a # at least one resolution, that is, "BE IT RESOLVED THAT" clauses which specify the actual action proposed by the motion. A motion with no resolutions is nothing.
    2. 3.1.3.b # motivation, that is, "WHEREAS" clauses which explain the reasoning or background for the motion. This is common for main motions and uncommon for others.
    3. 3.1.3.c # a mover, that is, the person who proposed (moved) the motion. All motions require this, even if implicitly.
    4. 3.1.3.d # a second, that is, an affirmative indication that a second person (the seconder) wishes to consider the motion. Most motions without a second fail without debate, because obviously the mover of the motion is the only person who wishes to consider it. Some motions, however, do not require a second, such as "move in camera", because by nature only the mover has prior reason to consider them.
    5. 3.1.3.e # a reading, that is, the Speaker reads out the full text of the motion, including any WHEREAS/BIRT clauses.
    6. 3.1.3.f # debate, that is, discussion of the merits of the motion. Most subsidiary and incidental motions appear during debate. Some motions are undebatable, usually because debate would defeat the purpose, such as "vote now". See section 3.2
    7. 3.1.3.g # a majority vote, that is, strictly greater than half of the Board agree that the action should be taken. See section 3.3
  4. 3.1.4 # Once a motion has been voted on, it has been disposed of.
    1. 3.1.4.a # If it passed, it may be undone by a main motion "to rescind" the motion; if it failed, it may not be proposed again until the next meeting.
    2. 3.1.4.b # Anyone who voted on the winning side may also move "to reconsider" the vote, which, when seconded, temporarily prevents the motion from taking effect. See section 3.7
    3. 3.1.4.c # Some subsidiary, incidental, or privileged motions have the effect of killing the motion(s) to which they apply (at least for the Board's purposes). In those cases, the vote on the additional motion disposes of both the additional motion and the motion(s) to which it applies.
  5. 3.1.5 # Any EngSoc Member may move or second any motion at both Board and General Meetings, and vote on any motion at General Meetings; only members of the Board may vote on motions at Board Meetings.

3.2 # Debate

  1. 3.2.1 # Debate is never required, and if nobody speaks, the motion moves directly to voting.
  2. 3.2.2 # Usually the mover is invited to speak in favor of their motion before anyone else is recognized.
  3. 3.2.3 # Any Member wishing to be recognized should raise their hand and display one of the following gestures:
    1. 3.2.3.a # Raised index finger (known as a "1") - if they wish to introduce a new point or subtopic of discussion, or make a subsidiary or incidental motion (see section 3.5) that is not part of the ongoing conversation
    2. 3.2.3.b # Raised index and middle fingers (V sign, known as a "2") - if they wish to continue the ongoing conversation by responding to a point made by another member (the one they are pointing to)
    3. 3.2.3.c # Raised pinky finger (known as a "pinky") - if they wish to make a point of privilege (see section 3.6.4) or order (see section 3.5.1) that is not so urgent as to justify interrupting the current speech
  4. 3.2.4 # In the normal course of debate, no one may speak until recognized by the Speaker, who must recognize pinkies first, and otherwise will normally recognize 2's before 1's.
    1. 3.2.4.a # The Board has a tendency to speak in circles. While it is not appropriate for the Speaker to move the previous question (see section 3.5.7) or to limit debate (see section 3.5.9) themselves, if it seems most of the Board are tired of the discussion, they may judiciously remind the Board that those options exist or possibly recognize a 1 in preference to entertaining further 2's on the same topic.
  5. 3.2.5 # Any debate should normally be confined to the merits of the motion, and the Speaker should pay attention to the content of any speech and interrupt if it seems it is becoming irrelevant to the discussion.
    1. 3.2.5.a # Note that "the merits of the motion" usually refers to the motion immediately pending, which may not be the main motion; thus, for example, debate on the motion to commit (see section 3.5.10) is limited to the propriety of committing the motion, and should not stray into the merits of the motion being committed.
  6. 3.2.6 # EngSoc Standing Rules remove all limits on debate, such that any Member may speak as long and as often as they wish without standing. Limits may be reintroduced by motion (see section 3.5.9).

3.3 # Voting

  1. 3.3.1 # Most motions require a majority vote of those present to pass. A majority means **>**50%; it does not mean ≥(50%+1) or other misconceived formulas. A tie vote fails, unless the Speaker casts a tie-breaking vote (which they may only do in General Meetings, where they have a vote). Members abstaining or not present are not counted; thus a vote of 3 for, 2 against, 5 abstaining, and 11 absent passes, while a vote of 5 for, 5 against, and 11 absent fails.
  2. 3.3.2 # The normal method of taking a vote is:
    1. 3.3.2.a # Say, "Those in favor raise their hands."
    2. 3.3.2.b # Count the number of members (not hands) in favor, chastising any ineligible voters. In Board Meetings, each name should be written down.
    3. 3.3.2.c # Say, "Hands down. Those against raise their hands."
    4. 3.3.2.d # Count those against, and record names if applicable.
    5. 3.3.2.e # Say, "All others abstain. Motion passes 5 to 3," or "Everyone else abstains. Motion fails 2 to 6," as the case may be.
  3. 3.3.3 # Debate is not allowed during voting; all debate must conclude (or have been closed) before voting occurs.
  4. 3.3.4 # Often instead of asking each Board member to vote, the Speaker may ask for "unanimous consent", meaning that everyone is assumed to agree unless any Board member wishes to see a full vote. (The request to see a full vote is itself a motion, to "divide the assembly", and is one of the few motions which does not require a vote.)
  5. 3.3.5 # Some motions require more than a majority, such as a ⅔ or supermajority vote, or a 100% or unanimous vote, or in rare cases a majority of the membership, in which case absences and abstentions are the same as "no" votes. These motions are this way usually because they prejudice the rights of one or more members; as such, they tend to be more important and unanimous consent should be used with them less often.

3.4 # Main Motions

  1. 3.4.1 # Section 2.3 and section 2.4.2 list the common types of main motions which appear before the Board and Annual General Meeting, respectively. Those motions are guaranteed to appear in their respective meetings, if applicable.
  2. 3.4.2 # Motions may be added to the agenda of any meeting if they are emailed to the Speaker and the Speaker does not rule them out of order, that is, invalid.
  3. 3.4.3 # Motions may also be added to the agenda during the meeting by means of an incidental motion to amend the agenda. Amending the agenda requires a majority vote before it is adopted at the beginning of the meeting, or a unanimous vote afterwards.
  4. 3.4.4 # A motion to reconsider (see section 3.7) that is pending because it could not be immediately considered may be called up in between main motions, instead of proceeding to the next main motion on the agenda. (If the motion being reconsidered had any privilege over main motions, then the consideration of its reconsideration has that same privilege).
  5. 3.4.5 # A motion that was tabled (see section 3.5.6) may be taken from the table in between main motions, instead of proceeding to the next main motion on the agenda.

3.5 # Subsidiary and Incidental Motions

  1. 3.5.1 # A point of order is essentially a request to the Speaker that they make a ruling on the Bylaws or rules of order. It is not a motion per se, and does not require a second, and anyone may interrupt anyone else to make a point of order. For example, one might interrupt and say, "Point of order - is this germane?" and the Speaker would then decide whether the ongoing speech was germane. The Speaker may also defer the decision to the Board, in which case it is treated as the Speaker answering "yes" followed by an appeal.
  2. 3.5.2 # The motion to appeal the decision of the chair is an incidental motion which:
    1. 3.5.2.a # can only be made immediately after the Speaker makes a decision.
    2. 3.5.2.b # opens up the question which the Speaker decided to instead be decided by the Board, in the form "Shall the Speaker's decision be reversed?"
    3. 3.5.2.c # requires a second and a supermajority vote (where if the motion passes, the Speaker's decision is reversed - note that this is the opposite of Robert's Rules!).
    4. 3.5.2.d # is undebatable if the decision has to do with decorum or if the pending motion is undebatable. However, the Speaker can always state their reasons for their decision, and if the appeal is debatable, they can also make one final statement answering the debate before moving the appeal to voting.
    5. 3.5.2.e # is unamendable.
  3. 3.5.3 # The motion to suspend the rules interfering with some action is an incidental motion which:
    1. 3.5.3.a # suspends any rules contained in Robert's Rules or the Standing Rules which interfere with accomplishing the proposed action.
    2. 3.5.3.b # cannot suspend any rules that protect absentees, such as notice requirements, or that protect the privacy of individuals, such as the Standing Rules on in camera sessions.
    3. 3.5.3.c # requires a second and:
      1. 3.5.3.c.i # a majority vote, if the rules being suspended exist only in the Standing Rules; or
      2. 3.5.3.c.ii # a supermajority vote, if the rules being suspended exist in Robert's Rules; or
      3. 3.5.3.c.iii # one of the above plus consent of the minorities protected by the rules, if applicable; or
      4. 3.5.3.c.iv # unanimous consent (for trivial suspensions, this is usually the easiest, but remember that some rules cannot be suspended even unanimously).
    4. 3.5.3.d # is undebatable and unamendable.
  4. 3.5.4 # The motion to object to the consideration of a main motion is an incidental motion which:
    1. 3.5.4.a # can only be made immediately after a main motion has just been read out loud by the Speaker, interrupting someone else who has just begun to speak if necessary.
    2. 3.5.4.b # immediately kills the motion. This motion is only useful if at least two EngSoc Members, but less than ⅓ of Board members present, wish to consider the motion. If only one person wishes to consider the motion, it can be killed simply by not seconding it. But if more than one person wishes to consider it, yet none of the Board do, then this motion is appropriate.
    3. 3.5.4.c # does not require a second, but requires a supermajority vote against considering the main motion (that is, a vote of less than ⅓ in favor kills the main motion).
    4. 3.5.4.d # is undebatable and unamendable.
  5. 3.5.5 # Any subsidiary motion listed here cannot be moved if one listed earlier is pending.
  6. 3.5.6 # The motion to "lay on the table" or simply to table another motion is a subsidiary motion which:
    1. 3.5.6.a # puts aside the other motion until it is taken up again. If it is not taken up by the end of the next meeting, it dies.
      1. 3.5.6.a.i # If the Board wishes to guarantee that it will be taken up again at a certain point, they should move to postpone definitely instead - see section 3.5.9
      2. 3.5.6.a.ii # When a main motion is tabled, any subsidiary motions pending against it are tabled with it; when the main motion is taken up, so too are the pending subsidiary motions.
    2. 3.5.6.b # requires a second and a majority vote.
    3. 3.5.6.c # is undebatable and unamendable.
  7. 3.5.7 # The motion to "call the question" or "close debate" or "vote now", known formally as the previous question, is a subsidiary motion which:
    1. 3.5.7.a # closes debate on the immediately pending motion, or any number of consecutively pending motions specified by the mover, and prompts an immediate vote on the motion(s) without further discussion.
      1. 3.5.7.a.i # For example, if a motion to commit is pending on the main motion, and a motion to amend is pending on the commitment, passing a motion to "call the question" means only the amendment will be immediately voted on; passing a motion to "call the question on the main motion" means the amendment, then the commitment, then finally the main motion will all be voted on in sequence without debate.
      2. 3.5.7.a.ii # If it is desired to instead reduce discussion without completely eliminating it, consider suggesting the motion to limit debate - see section 3.5.8
    2. 3.5.7.b # requires a second and a supermajority vote.
    3. 3.5.7.c # is undebatable and unamendable.
  8. 3.5.8 # The motion to limit debate or "extend limits of debate" or "change limits of debate" is a subsidiary motion which:
    1. 3.5.8.a # introduces limits on the frequency, duration, timing, subject, and/or any other properties of further debate, for the duration of the immediately pending motion and any other specified motions. For example, the Board could limit debate such that:
      1. 3.5.8.a.i # direct responses (2's) are no longer recognized, and everyone simply speaks in the order in which they raised their hand;
      2. 3.5.8.a.ii # no one may speak a second time until everyone else who wishes to speak has had their turn;
      3. 3.5.8.a.iii # speeches are limited to one minute each; and/or
      4. 3.5.8.a.iv # no one may speak for more than ten minutes in total.
    2. 3.5.8.b # requires a second and a supermajority vote.
    3. 3.5.8.c # is undebatable, and amendable only as to the specific limits.
  9. 3.5.9 # The motion to postpone definitely or simply to "postpone" another motion is a subsidiary motion which:
    1. 3.5.9.a # puts aside the other motion until the time specified in the motion to postpone definitely. For example a motion could be postponed until a certain time of day, or until the end of the meeting, or to the next meeting (but not the one after that). Like tabling, pending subsidiary motions are postponed along with a main motion.
    2. 3.5.9.b # requires a second and a majority vote.
    3. 3.5.9.c # is debatable only as to the propriety of postponing the motion, and is amendable only as to the time to which to postpone the motion.
  10. 3.5.10 # The motion to commit or "refer" another motion is a subsidiary motion which:
    1. 3.5.10.a # sends the motion to a committee (either a Standing Committee in Bylaw 1 section 4.12 or a special committee) for the committee to consider. Unlike tabling or postponing, only pending amendments are committed along with a main motion.
    2. 3.5.10.b # has the effect of killing the motion as far as the Board is concerned, since the committee can choose not to bring the motion back; if they do bring it back, it is brought as a new motion.
    3. 3.5.10.c # requires a second and a majority vote.
    4. 3.5.10.d # is debatable only as to the propriety of committing the motion, and is amendable only as to the committee to which to commit the motion (and its composition, if a special committee).
  11. 3.5.11 # The motion to amend another motion is a subsidiary motion which:
    1. 3.5.11.a # changes the motivation or resolutions contained in the other motion.
    2. 3.5.11.b # is not allowed to make such a drastic change that the other motion becomes a substantively different motion.
    3. 3.5.11.c # requires a second and a majority vote.
    4. 3.5.11.d # is debatable only when the other motion is debatable.
    5. 3.5.11.e # is amendable only if the motion it amends is not a motion to amend. That is, a main motion may be amended, and that amendment too, but not the second degree amendment.
  12. 3.5.12 # Other subsidiary and incidental motions exist, but are so rare in EngSoc that they are not worth listing here; they can be found in Robert's Rules.

3.6 # Privileged Motions

  1. 3.6.1 # Any privileged motion listed here cannot be moved if one listed earlier is pending.
  2. 3.6.2 # The motion to adjourn is a privileged motion which:
    1. 3.6.2.a # immediately ends the meeting. If the motion specifies the time to which to adjourn, the meeting continues then, but in the interim it still stands "adjourned" for the purposes of the Bylaws.
    2. 3.6.2.b # requires a second and a majority vote.
    3. 3.6.2.c # is undebatable, and is amendable only as to the time to which to adjourn.
  3. 3.6.3 # The motion to take a recess is a privileged motion which:
    1. 3.6.3.a # immediately suspends the meeting for the duration specified in the motion. The state of the meeting at the moment the recess begins is frozen until the recess ends, at which point the meeting picks back up as if nothing had happened. It is the Speaker's responsibility to bring the meeting back to order when the end of recess arrives.
    2. 3.6.3.b # requires a second and a majority vote.
    3. 3.6.3.c # is undebatable, and is amendable only as to the end of the recess.
  4. 3.6.4 # A point of privilege is essentially a way of pointing out that something is wrong with the meeting itself in some way, for example the lights have gone off or not everyone can hear the person speaking. It can interrupt someone else if it is urgent enough, but not if a motion to adjourn or recess is pending; otherwise it should be indicated with a pinky finger.

3.7 # Reconsideration

  1. 3.7.1 # As stated in section 3.1.4.b, proposing that the vote be reconsidered, in itself, temporarily restrains the motion if the motion to reconsider is seconded.
  2. 3.7.2 # Moving the reconsideration must be done within the day the vote happened or the next business day, and can be done even if someone else is speaking. However, considering the reconsideration can only happen if and when the original motion could be considered; if another motion is ongoing, then the reconsideration remains pending until it is called up (see section 3.4.4).
  3. 3.7.3 # A motion cannot be reconsidered if some other available motion would have the same effect, or if the motion can no longer be undone. For example, if the Board votes to sign a contract and the other party is notified that the contract is signed the same night, it is too late to reconsider the vote even if the next business day has not yet passed; and if the Board votes to amend the Bylaws, there is no point reconsidering the vote because the amendment can be undone by another amendment.
  4. 3.7.4 # Debate on the motion to reconsider is allowed to the same extent that debate on the motion being reconsidered was allowed.
  5. 3.7.5 # No matter what the vote requirement for the original motion was, reconsideration of it only requires a majority vote.
  6. 3.7.6 # If the motion to reconsider passes, the original motion is immediately back on the table as if the vote had never happened. It can then pass or fail from that point. If the motion to reconsider fails, the original motion takes effect again and no further reconsideration may be proposed.
